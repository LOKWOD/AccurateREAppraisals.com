HISTORICAL VALUATION

Retrospective Residential Appraisal Guide

Retrospective appraisal is reconstruction. The appraiser identifies what the property was, what the market was doing and what information typical participants had on the historical effective date.

Updated August 17, 2026. Educational information only; not legal, tax, lending, engineering or accounting advice.

Lock the date before research

Date of death, separation, transfer, damage or another event may control the assignment. Confirm it from the appropriate source and state the intended use.

Changing the date changes the relevant market and may change property condition. It is a substantive scope change, not a formatting edit.

Build a historical property file

Collect archived listings, photographs, permits, plans, insurance records, prior appraisals and improvement dates. A current inspection cannot by itself establish past condition.

Identify later additions, renovations, damage and demolition. Unverified historical facts should be treated transparently rather than assumed silently.

Recover market evidence from the period

Research sales, listings, market trends, financing conditions and local influences around the effective date. Contract and closing dates may both matter.

Archived listing detail can be critical for rural, waterfront and custom properties because public records often omit quality, view and accessory improvements.

Explain source limitations

Historical records may be incomplete, changed or inconsistent. The report should identify sources, verification and the effect of uncertainty.

A broad index or current sale adjusted backward may provide context but usually does not replace property-specific analysis.

Match the report to its purpose

Estate, tax, litigation and financial-planning uses may require different intended users and reporting depth. Confirm those requirements before engagement.

The historical opinion is not current market value and should not be represented as today's lending or listing conclusion.

Assignment planning checklist

  • Confirm historical date
  • Identify intended use
  • Collect archived photos
  • Collect prior listings
  • Document improvement dates
  • Provide surveys and deeds
  • Identify later changes
  • Allow archival research
  • Disclose source limitations
  • Keep report use date-specific

Frequently asked questions

How old can the effective date be?

Feasibility depends on reliable records and market evidence.

Is a current inspection required?

It may be useful, but the scope depends on the assignment and historical evidence.

Can owner recollection be used?

It can be considered with appropriate verification and disclosure; it is not automatically conclusive.

Why can historical work cost more?

Archived research and reconstruction often require additional time and verification.

Need a property-specific scope?

Send the address, intended use, effective date and material property characteristics before scheduling.
